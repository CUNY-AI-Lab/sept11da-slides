Tier 1

Personally identifiable information

Machine reading makes an archive searchable. It also makes it searchable for things a depositor in 2002 never imagined being findable.

For example:

  • The depositor ticked No to consent
  • A minor who is directly reachable
  • An address or phone number spoken aloud in a recording
  • A full name and school written on a card
  • Contact details buried in a scanned document
Tier 2

Is the machine right?

An accuracy figure means nothing until enough has been checked. A hundred and fifty of a source is a defensible slice; ten is an anecdote. The model's original output is never overwritten — a corrected transcript is stored beside it, with who corrected it and when.

Tier 3

Metadata — the task that never got funded

Seventy thousand items. Eighteen fields. Twenty-four usable dates. Zero tags.

Everything above this line was engineering. This one is scholarship, and it is the one that decides what the archive can be asked.

What we built

The review interface

Every item comes up beside what the model said about it. A reviewer judges the match, fixes the text if it is wrong, and the model’s original output is kept either way.

Tier 4

What becomes askable once it is indexed.

What happens to plurality when the event acquires an established narrative? These are the questions an index makes answerable — and none of them were answerable last year.

Tier 4 · Lens 01

Naming the event over time

“September 11” · “9/11” · “the attacks” · “the towers” · “Ground Zero” — when does the shorthand consolidate?

Diachronic naming is the standard move in memory studies. What is unusual is the corpus: this is one of very few bodies of dated, ordinary, non-journalistic language about the event, written by people who were not being edited.

Tier 4 · Lens 02

Distance

Does what people write vary by how far they travelled to write it?

Map the hometowns, ring them by distance from Lower Manhattan, and ask the real question. A visitor from Nebraska and one from Jersey City are doing different things by standing in that room.

~20,000hometowns given by depositors
Tier 4 · Lens 03

Absence, measured rather than asserted

Until now nobody could responsibly say the archive under-represents anything — because nobody could count.

With an index you can finally count what isn't here: the backlash. Sikh and Arab American experience. The murder of Balbir Singh Sodhi. Dissent. Conspiracy. The wars that followed.

A claim about silence in an archive should be a measurement, not a hunch.

Tier 4 · Lens 04

Languages

An English-only reading hides whoever else is in there.

There is a Chinatown oral history collection in this archive. There is an Italian-language Flash newscast. Language detection across the full corpus is a single pass — and it is the difference between an archive of New York and an archive of English-speaking New York.

51Mcharacters now available to detect against
Tier 4 · Lens 05

Children's drawings

Towers, planes, flags, hearts — how did children draw something most of them only saw on television?

A sketch flag is recorded per page, and the pilot rate implies several thousand of them. Iconography over time, in a visual record nobody has been able to assemble because nobody could find the pages.

Tier 4 · Lens 06

The archived web as a link graph

What did the post-9/11 web actually point at?

Extract the hyperlinks out of the site captures and the shape of an early-2000s web neighbourhood comes back — who linked to whom, and what has since disappeared from the other end of those links.

The rule

Every visual walks back to the artifact

If a chart says 6,761 cards in April 2003, clicking it gets you those cards.

  • ChartA count you can see — 6,761 cards, April 2003
  • ClickThe 6,761 cards themselves, not a summary of them
  • ClickOne card: the scan, beside its transcript, beside who corrected it

A visualization that cannot be walked back to the object is an assertion. The whole point of indexing a plural archive is that its claims stay auditable by the person reading them.
